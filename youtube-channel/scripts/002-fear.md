# Long video 002: What Happens to Your Body When You're Scared?

- **Length:** about 1,350 words, roughly 9 minutes with the pauses between voices
- **Story shape:** one scare, replayed in slow motion, second by second
- **Thumbnail:** "0.1 SECONDS" (Sam mid-scream, lit by the open cupboard)
- **Other titles:** "Your Body Panics Before You Know Why" / "What Fear Does to Your Body"

## The voices

This one is built to be entertaining, not a lecture: four voices that argue, joke and react, like Paper Billions.

| Name | Who | Voice to pick in ElevenLabs |
|---|---|---|
| NARRATOR | Tells the story | Calm, warm, a little amused |
| SAM | The guy who gets scared | Casual, young, a bit sarcastic |
| AMY | Sam's amygdala, the brain's panic alarm | Loud, fast, jumpy |
| CORTEX | Sam's thinking brain: clever but slow | Slow, posh, completely deadpan |

**How to record it:** in ElevenLabs, use the multi-voice option (Studio, or the dialogue feature), give each name its own voice, and export everything as **one MP3**. Leave the names out of the audio.

**The square-bracket cues** like `[screams]` are Eleven v3 audio tags. If your voice model reads them out loud instead of acting them, delete them.

**Pronunciation:** amygdala = "uh-MIG-duh-luh". Hypothalamus = "high-po-THAL-uh-mus".

## Script (paste this into ElevenLabs)

---

NARRATOR: This is Sam. It's two in the morning, and Sam is hungry.

SAM: [whispers] Just a little snack. Nobody needs to know.

NARRATOR: Sam opens the kitchen cupboard. It's dark in there. And on the top shelf... something moves.

SAM: [screams] AAAAH!

NARRATOR: Stop. Freeze it right there.

NARRATOR: In the next tenth of a second, Sam's body is going to rewrite his whole night. His heart, his lungs, his eyes, even his blood are about to change. And the strangest part? Sam won't know what he saw until it's all already happened.

NARRATOR: So let's rewind, and watch it in slow motion.

NARRATOR: Zero seconds. Light bounces off the thing in the cupboard and lands on the back of Sam's eyes.

NARRATOR: Normally, that picture takes the scenic route. It travels to the back of the brain, where the vision department slowly works out what it's looking at. Shape. Colour. Edges. Meaning.

CORTEX: Hmm. Interesting. Let us consider the options.

NARRATOR: That's Sam's cortex. Very smart. Very thorough. Very... slow.

NARRATOR: But there's a shortcut. A rough, blurry copy of the picture goes somewhere else, straight to a little almond-shaped lump deep inside the brain.

AMY: [shouting] SOMETHING MOVED!

NARRATOR: This is the amygdala. Amy, for short. Amy doesn't do details. Amy has one job: deciding whether something might kill you.

NARRATOR: And Amy is fast. In 2016, scientists recorded signals from electrodes inside the brains of hospital patients, and showed them photos of faces.

NARRATOR: When the face looked frightened, Amy started reacting seventy-four thousandths of a second after it appeared. That's faster than you can blink.

SAM: So my brain panics before it even knows what it's panicking about?

NARRATOR: Exactly. Amy shoots first, and asks questions never.

AMY: Better safe than eaten!

NARRATOR: Amy doesn't run the body herself. She slams the panic button on the hypothalamus, the brain's command centre.

AMY: CODE RED! CODE RED! EVERYBODY UP!

NARRATOR: The command centre fires a signal down a special set of nerves, all the way to two small glands sitting on top of Sam's kidneys. The adrenal glands.

NARRATOR: And they dump one chemical straight into his blood. Adrenaline.

NARRATOR: Now watch what adrenaline does to Sam. Department by department.

NARRATOR: Heart. Faster. Harder. Blood pressure goes up, and blood gets shoved out to the muscles.

SAM: Why is my heart doing a drum solo?

NARRATOR: Lungs. He breathes faster, and the tiny airways inside open wide to grab more oxygen.

NARRATOR: Eyes. The pupils get bigger, to let in more light. His senses get sharper.

NARRATOR: Fuel. Stored sugar and fat get emptied into his blood, so every muscle has energy to burn.

NARRATOR: And digestion? Cancelled. Anything the body doesn't need right now gets put on hold. Including that snack.

SAM: Not the snack.

NARRATOR: In about a second, Sam has gone from sleepy guy in pyjamas to the most alert, fully fuelled version of himself. Ready to fight. Or, more likely...

SAM: [screams] NOPE!

NARRATOR: Meanwhile, back in the vision department, Sam's cortex has finally finished thinking.

CORTEX: Having carefully reviewed the evidence... it appears to be... a sock.

AMY: ...A sock?

CORTEX: A sock. On the shelf. It fell over.

NARRATOR: Too late. Sam has already jumped backwards, knocked over a chair, and thrown a cereal box at the sock.

NARRATOR: And that's not a mistake. That's the whole point. The alarm goes off before the brain has fully worked out what it saw. It's the reason people can leap out of the way of a car before they even think about it.

NARRATOR: Amy will be wrong a hundred times. Socks. Shadows. Garden hoses. But the one time it really is a snake, being fast is what keeps you alive.

AMY: You're welcome.

NARRATOR: Fear also does some stranger things. Let's go through them.

NARRATOR: Goosebumps. Tiny muscles at the base of each hair pull it upright. On a furry animal, that fluffs up the whole coat. On Sam, it fluffs up... nothing.

SAM: I look like a plucked chicken.

NARRATOR: So why do humans still have it? In mice, Harvard scientists found that the same nerve and muscle that make goosebumps also help control hair growth. An old reflex, with a second job.

NARRATOR: Next. Time slows down. Ever had a scary moment that seemed to last forever? Neuroscientist David Eagleman tested it, by dropping volunteers backwards off a tower. Thirty-one metres, into a net.

SAM: Who signs up for that?

NARRATOR: Volunteers. The fall took two and a half seconds. Afterwards, people guessed it had lasted about a third longer.

NARRATOR: But here's the clever part. They also wore a screen on their wrist, flashing numbers too fast to read. If time really slowed down for them, they'd be able to read it in mid-air.

CORTEX: They could not.

NARRATOR: Time doesn't slow down. Your memory just writes down far more detail when you're scared, so afterwards, it feels longer.

NARRATOR: And bloodcurdling? That one's almost real. Dutch scientists sat twenty-four young volunteers in front of a horror film. On another day, the same people watched a calm documentary about champagne.

SAM: I'd volunteer for the champagne one.

NARRATOR: After the horror film, a protein that helps blood clot had gone up. No actual clots. But their blood was getting ready, just in case.

NARRATOR: For Sam, the danger was a sock, so it's over in seconds. The adrenaline fades, and the body's brakes kick in to calm everything down. That's why, after a big scare, you feel shaky and drained.

NARRATOR: But if the danger doesn't go away, the brain sends a second, slower wave: a relay of hormones that ends with one called cortisol. Cortisol keeps the gas pedal pressed down.

NARRATOR: That's useful for a few minutes. It's not useful for weeks. When the alarm never switches off, the system built to save your life starts wearing it down.

SAM: Okay, but can you actually be scared to death?

NARRATOR: Very rarely, a sudden flood of stress hormones can stun the heart. Doctors call it broken heart syndrome. The heart muscle suddenly weakens, usually for a short time. It's real. But it's rare.

NARRATOR: Which raises the obvious question. If fear is this intense... why do people pay money for it?

SAM: Haunted houses. Horror films. Roller coasters.

NARRATOR: Scientists in Denmark put heart-rate monitors on a hundred and ten visitors to a haunted house, and filmed them at every jump scare.

AMY: [screams] AAAAH!

NARRATOR: They found a sweet spot. Too little fear, and people were bored. Too much, and they stopped having fun. Right in the middle, scared but safe, is where people enjoyed it most.

NARRATOR: Your body gets the full alarm. Your cortex knows the zombie is a guy called Kevin on minimum wage. And somewhere between the two, it's fun.

CORTEX: Kevin is an excellent zombie.

NARRATOR: Back to Sam's kitchen. Heart slowing down. Breathing back to normal. Sock on the floor.

SAM: Okay. Nobody saw that.

NARRATOR: Everything you just watched, the alarm, the adrenaline, the heart, the lungs, the eyes, started before Sam knew what he was looking at.

NARRATOR: Your body is always one step ahead of you. Most of the time, it's wrong.

AMY: But not every time.

NARRATOR: And the one time it's right, it doesn't wait for permission.

AMY: [whispers] ...Something moved.

SAM: Nope. Nope. Nope.

## Check before publishing

- "Faster than you can blink": a blink is commonly given as 100 to 400 milliseconds; I have not found the source for that range yet.
- The 2016 patients were having electrodes placed for epilepsy treatment, which is the usual reason for these recordings; the abstract I read does not say so, so the script only says "hospital patients".
- "On a furry animal, that fluffs up the whole coat" and the cat puffing up are common knowledge, not from the sources above.
- The haunted house was in Denmark (Aarhus University researchers); confirm the location from the paper's method section.
- Kevin the zombie is a joke, not a real person from the study.
