# Long video 002: What Happens to Your Body When You're Scared?

- **Voice:** one narrator only (rewritten on 20 November 2026 from the first four-voice version, to match the channel decision of 17 November)
- **Length:** about 1,380 words, roughly 8 minutes
- **Story shape:** one scare, replayed in slow motion, second by second
- **Thumbnail:** "0.1 SECONDS" (Sam mid-scream, lit by the open cupboard)
- **Other titles:** "Your Body Panics Before You Know Why" / "What Fear Does to Your Body"

## How one voice carries four characters

Sam (the guy who gets scared), Amy (his amygdala, the brain's panic alarm) and Cortex (his thinking brain: clever, slow, deadpan) are still on screen and still "talk", but only in speech bubbles. The narrator reads or reports their lines ("Amy whispers: something moved"), and their mouths move with the bubbles. Amy's bubbles are big, red and shaky; Cortex's are neat and grey.

**Delivery notes for ElevenLabs:** warm and amused, like replaying a friend's embarrassing moment frame by frame. Speed up for the adrenaline section, slow down for cortisol and broken heart syndrome. Blank lines are pauses.

**Pronunciation:** amygdala = "uh-MIG-duh-luh". Hypothalamus = "high-po-THAL-uh-mus". Eagleman = "EE-gul-man".

## Narration (paste this into ElevenLabs)

---

This is Sam. It's two in the morning, and Sam is hungry.

He creeps into the kitchen, whispering to himself. Just a little snack. Nobody needs to know.

Sam opens the kitchen cupboard. It's dark in there. And on the top shelf... something moves.

Sam screams.

Stop. Freeze it right there.

In the next tenth of a second, Sam's body is going to rewrite his whole night. His heart, his lungs, his eyes, even his blood are about to change. And the strangest part? Sam won't know what he saw until it's all already happened.

So let's rewind, and watch it in slow motion.

Zero seconds. Light bounces off the thing in the cupboard, and lands on the back of Sam's eyes.

Normally, that picture takes the scenic route. It travels to the back of the brain, where the vision department slowly works out what it's looking at. Shape. Colour. Edges. Meaning.

Meet Sam's cortex. Very smart. Very thorough. Very... slow. Right now it's stroking its chin, saying: hmm, interesting, let us consider the options.

But there's a shortcut. A rough, blurry copy of the picture goes somewhere else, straight to a little almond-shaped lump deep inside the brain.

And this one is already shouting. Something moved!

This is the amygdala. Let's call her Amy. Amy doesn't do details. Amy has one job: deciding whether something might kill you.

And Amy is fast. In 2016, scientists recorded signals from electrodes inside the brains of hospital patients, and showed them photos of faces.

When the face looked frightened, Amy started reacting seventy-four thousandths of a second after it appeared. That's faster than you can blink.

So, wait. Sam's brain panics before it even knows what it's panicking about?

Exactly. Amy shoots first, and asks questions never. Her motto: better safe than eaten.

Amy doesn't run the body herself. She slams the panic button on the hypothalamus, the brain's command centre. Code red. Code red. Everybody up.

The command centre fires a signal down a special set of nerves, all the way to two small glands sitting on top of Sam's kidneys. The adrenal glands.

And they dump one chemical straight into his blood. Adrenaline.

Now watch what adrenaline does to Sam. Department by department.

Heart. Faster. Harder. Blood pressure goes up, and blood gets shoved out to the muscles. Sam's heart is doing a drum solo.

Lungs. He breathes faster, and the tiny airways inside open wide to grab more oxygen.

Eyes. The pupils get bigger, to let in more light. His senses get sharper.

Fuel. Stored sugar and fat get emptied into his blood, so every muscle has energy to burn.

And digestion? Cancelled. Anything the body doesn't need right now gets put on hold. Including that snack.

Not the snack.

In about a second, Sam has gone from sleepy guy in pyjamas to the most alert, fully fuelled version of himself. Ready to fight. Or, much more likely, to run.

Meanwhile, back in the vision department, the cortex has finally finished thinking.

Having carefully reviewed the evidence, it has reached a conclusion. It's a sock.

A sock. On the shelf. It fell over.

Amy blinks. A sock?

Too late. Sam has already jumped backwards, knocked over a chair, and thrown a cereal box at the sock.

And that's not a mistake. That's the whole point. The alarm goes off before the brain has fully worked out what it saw. It's the reason people can leap out of the way of a car before they even think about it.

Amy will be wrong a hundred times. Socks. Shadows. Garden hoses. But the one time it really is a snake, being fast is what keeps you alive.

You're welcome, says Amy.

Fear also does some stranger things. Let's go through them.

Pale as a ghost. That one's real. Blood gets moved away from the skin, so your hands go cold and clammy. Some people go white. Some go red. Sam manages both.

Pain on mute. In real danger, the body turns pain right down. It's one reason people in car accidents often don't feel their injuries until afterwards, once they're safe.

The shakes. All that adrenaline leaves your muscles tense and twitchy, ready to move at any moment. So they twitch.

And in a truly terrifying moment, some people lose control of their bladder. As far as your body is concerned, that's just not a priority right now.

Sam, thankfully, was only scared by a sock.

Goosebumps. Tiny muscles at the base of each hair pull it upright. On a furry animal, that fluffs up the whole coat. On Sam, it fluffs up... nothing. He looks like a plucked chicken.

So why do humans still have it? In mice, Harvard scientists found that the same nerve and muscle that make goosebumps also help control hair growth. An old reflex, with a second job.

Next. Time slows down. Ever had a scary moment that seemed to last forever? Neuroscientist David Eagleman tested it, by dropping volunteers backwards off a tower. Thirty-one metres, into a net.

Who signs up for that? Volunteers, apparently.

The fall took two and a half seconds. Afterwards, people guessed it had lasted about a third longer.

But here's the clever part. They also wore a screen on their wrist, flashing numbers too fast to read. If time really slowed down for them, they'd be able to read it in mid-air.

They could not.

Time doesn't slow down. Your memory just writes down far more detail when you're scared, so afterwards, it feels longer.

And bloodcurdling? That one's almost real. Dutch scientists sat twenty-four young volunteers in front of a horror film. On another day, the same people watched a calm documentary about champagne.

Honestly, the champagne day sounds like the better deal.

After the horror film, a protein that helps blood clot had gone up. No actual clots. But their blood was getting ready, just in case.

For Sam, the danger was a sock, so it's over in seconds. The adrenaline fades, and the body's brakes kick in to calm everything down. That's why, after a big scare, you feel shaky and drained. It can take twenty to thirty minutes for your body to fully settle down.

But if the danger doesn't go away, the brain sends a second, slower wave: a relay of hormones that ends with one called cortisol. Cortisol keeps the gas pedal pressed down.

That's useful for a few minutes. It's not useful for weeks. When the alarm never switches off, the system built to save your life starts wearing it down.

Which brings us to the question Sam is now asking. Can you actually be scared to death?

Very rarely, a sudden flood of stress hormones can stun the heart. Doctors call it broken heart syndrome. The heart muscle suddenly weakens, usually for a short time. It's real. But it's rare.

Which raises the obvious question. If fear is this intense, why do people pay money for it? Haunted houses. Horror films. Roller coasters.

Scientists in Denmark put heart-rate monitors on a hundred and ten visitors to a haunted house, and filmed them at every jump scare.

They found a sweet spot. Too little fear, and people were bored. Too much, and they stopped having fun. Right in the middle, scared but safe, is where people enjoyed it most.

Your body gets the full alarm. Your cortex knows the zombie is a guy called Kevin on minimum wage. And somewhere between the two, it's fun.

For the record, the cortex would like to add that Kevin is an excellent zombie.

Back to Sam's kitchen. Heart slowing down. Breathing back to normal. Sock on the floor.

Sam looks around. Okay. Nobody saw that.

Everything you just watched, the alarm, the adrenaline, the heart, the lungs, the eyes, started before Sam knew what he was looking at.

Your body is always one step ahead of you. Most of the time, it's wrong.

But not every time.

And the one time it's right, it doesn't wait for permission.

Somewhere in the dark, a cupboard door creaks.

Amy whispers. Something moved.

Nope.

# Shorts from video 002 (one narrator)

## Short 1: Your brain panics before you know why

Your brain decides you're scared before you know what you're looking at.

That's your amygdala. In a 2016 study, it started reacting to a frightened face just seventy-four thousandths of a second after it appeared.

The part of your brain that actually works out what you saw? Still thinking. It gets there in the end. It's a sock.

Too late. You've already jumped.

## Short 2: Time doesn't slow down when you're scared

Scary moments feel like slow motion. So a scientist dropped people backwards off a tower to test it. Volunteers, apparently.

The fall took two and a half seconds. People remembered it as a third longer. But a screen on their wrist flashed numbers too fast to read, and mid-fall they still couldn't read it.

Time doesn't slow down. Your memory just records more, so it feels longer afterwards.

## Short 3: Horror films change your blood

"Bloodcurdling" might be more real than you think.

Scientists showed twenty-four volunteers a horror film, and on another day, a documentary about champagne.

After the horror film, a protein that helps blood clot had gone up. No real clots, but their blood was getting ready, just in case.

## Short 4: Why do you get goosebumps?

When you're scared or cold, tiny muscles yank every hair on your body upright.

On a furry animal, that fluffs up the coat. On you, it does almost nothing.

But Harvard scientists found, in mice, that the same nerve and muscle also control hair growth. So the reflex never went away. It just got a second job.
