---

Every few months, a headline tells you that scientists have found another Earth.

A second home. Earth two point zero.

And every time, nobody tells you the catch.

So today we're going to pick the best candidate we have, the single most Earth-like planet anyone knows about, and ask three simple questions. Where is it? What is it actually like there? And could a human being ever live on it?

The answers are stranger than the headlines.

Let's start with why you keep hearing about a new Earth.

Astronomers have now confirmed more than six thousand planets around other stars. Almost all of them are terrible. Balls of gas hotter than a furnace. Frozen rocks in the dark. Worlds where it may rain molten glass.

So when one turns up that is roughly the size of Earth, and sits at a distance from its star where water could stay liquid, it makes the news. That distance has a name: the habitable zone. Not too hot, not too cold.

But here is the first catch. Habitable zone does not mean habitable. Venus sits near the edge of our Sun's habitable zone, and its surface is hot enough to melt lead. The zone only tells you a planet has a chance. It is a ticket to the lottery, not the prize.

So which planet holds the best ticket?

It's called TRAPPIST-1e.

It was announced in twenty seventeen, one of seven rocky planets circling the same small star. It is ninety-two percent as wide as Earth. Its gravity is about four fifths of ours, so a person who weighs eighty kilograms here would feel like sixty-five there. And it receives about two thirds of the sunlight Earth does.

Here is the number that gets scientists excited. Take away Earth's air, and our planet would average about minus eighteen degrees Celsius. Do the same sum for TRAPPIST-1e and you get about minus twenty-three. On paper, these two worlds start from almost the same place.

On paper.

Because now we have to talk about its sun.

The star TRAPPIST-1 is a red dwarf. It has nine percent of the mass of our Sun, and it is barely wider than the planet Jupiter. It glows a dim salmon red.

A star that weak only warms what is very close to it. So TRAPPIST-1e huddles in tight, thirty times closer to its star than Earth is to the Sun. One trip around takes six days.

That's a year. Six days. If you are thirty years old on Earth, you'd be about eighteen hundred on TRAPPIST-1e. You would have a birthday every week, and frankly you'd stop buying cake.

Being that close has a second effect. The star's gravity has almost certainly locked the planet in place, the same way Earth has locked the Moon. One side always faces the star. The other side never does.

So on TRAPPIST-1e the sun does not rise, and it does not set. It hangs in one spot in the sky forever, about four times wider than our Sun looks to us. One half of the planet lives in permanent afternoon. The other half lives in a night that never ends.

And the star is not gentle. Red dwarfs throw off violent flares, bursts of radiation that can peel the air off a nearby planet, layer by layer, over billions of years.

Which brings us to the only question that really matters.

Does TRAPPIST-1e still have air?

Without an atmosphere, there is no pressure to hold water as a liquid. No blanket to move heat from the day side to the night side. Nothing to breathe. Without air, this is not Earth two point zero. It is a rock.

So how do you check for air on a planet you cannot even see, forty light-years away?

You wait for it to cross in front of its star.

When it does, a tiny sliver of starlight passes through the planet's atmosphere on its way to us, if there is an atmosphere. Each gas swallows its own particular colours of light. So the missing colours work like a fingerprint. Find the gaps, and you know what the air is made of.

The problem is the size of the signal. For a planet this small, the change in the star's light is a few thousandths of one percent. It is like trying to notice a single gnat crossing a searchlight, from another city.

Only one machine can do it. The James Webb Space Telescope, floating one and a half million kilometres from Earth, with a golden mirror six and a half metres across.

Webb has now watched TRAPPIST-1e cross its star four times. Here is what it found.

First, the planet does not have a thick hydrogen atmosphere, the puffy kind it was probably born with. The star's flares would have blown that away long ago, and they did.

Second, it is unlikely to have air dominated by carbon dioxide, like Venus or Mars.

That leaves two possibilities standing. One: a heavier atmosphere rich in nitrogen, the same gas that makes up most of the air in your lungs right now. Two: no air at all. Bare rock.

And the scientists who ran the study say the odds are about even.

That is the honest state of Earth two point zero. Fifty fifty. A coin that is still in the air.

It is not the only contender. Forty-eight light-years away sits LHS 1140 b, a bigger world about seventy percent wider than Earth. A new study in the journal Science reports that a telescope in Chile caught helium gas streaming off it in twenty twenty-four, a sign it may hold a thin atmosphere. But when the team looked again in twenty twenty-five, the signal was not there. Maybe it faded. Maybe it was hiding in the noise.

That is what this search really looks like. Not a photograph of a blue planet. A faint wobble in a line of data, and a group of very careful people refusing to say more than they know.

But let's be generous. Let's say the coin lands well. TRAPPIST-1e has air, and somewhere on it, liquid water.

Could humans live there?

Start with the weather. With one side always facing the star, the best real estate may be the ring between day and night, a strip of endless sunset running all the way around the planet. If there is an ocean, it might be a single round sea directly under the star, surrounded by ice.

Above you, the six sister planets would drift across the sky, some of them appearing larger than our Moon.

It would be beautiful. It would also be bathed in radiation every time the star flared. You would not walk outside. You would live under shielding, or underground.

And that is assuming you could arrive. Which is the last catch, and the biggest.

TRAPPIST-1 is forty light-years away. Light itself, the fastest thing there is, takes forty years to make the trip.

We are much slower than light.

The fastest thing humans have ever sent out of the solar system is Voyager 1. It launched in nineteen seventy-seven and has been flying ever since at about seventeen kilometres every second. This month, after forty-nine years, it reaches a milestone. It is now one light-day from Earth. One day of light's travel.

TRAPPIST-1 is more than fourteen thousand light-days away.

At Voyager's speed, the journey takes over seven hundred thousand years. Our entire species has existed for about three hundred thousand. You would need to leave twice as long ago as there have been humans, and you still would not be there yet.

So no. Nobody is moving to Earth two point zero. Not us, not our grandchildren, not anyone we can imagine.

Then why does any of this matter?

Because the real prize was never a place to move to.

Right now, Webb is in the middle of fifteen more observations of TRAPPIST-1e, timed so that it crosses its star right after a neighbouring planet that we are fairly sure is bare rock. Compare the two, subtract the star's noise, and whatever is left over is the planet's air.

When those results arrive, we may know, for the first time in history, whether a planet the size of ours, in the right place around another star, has an atmosphere.

If it does, the galaxy just got a lot more interesting. Red dwarfs are the most common stars there are. If their planets can hold on to air, there could be billions of places worth checking.

And if it doesn't? One of the scientists on the team put it best. Finding nothing could teach us that we are special here on Earth, and to love our planet even more.

Either way, there is no Earth two point zero we can reach. There is one planet we know of with air you can breathe, water you can drink, and a sun that actually sets.

You are standing on it.
