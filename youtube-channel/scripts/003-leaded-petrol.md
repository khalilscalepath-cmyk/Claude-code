# Long video 003: This Chemical Was in Everyone's Fuel

- **Length:** about 1,150 spoken words, roughly 8 minutes with the pauses between voices
- **Story shape:** rise and fall: a miracle fix, a cover-up, a scientist who wouldn't stop, a ban, and the bill
- **Thumbnail:** a 1920s petrol pump labelled "ETHYL" with a red "BANNED" stamp (option B: the inventor sniffing a bottle, "HE SNIFFED IT")
- **Other titles:** "The Poison in Every Car for 70 Years" / "The Man Who Put Lead in Your Petrol"

## Script (paste this into ElevenLabs)

---

NARRATOR: In 1924, a chemist stood in front of a room full of reporters. He poured a poison over his hands. Then he held the bottle under his nose, and breathed it in. For a whole minute.

MIDGLEY: See? Perfectly safe. I could do this every day.

NARRATOR: He could not. Not long after, he had to take time off work. He had lead poisoning.

SAM: Wait. So he lost?

NARRATOR: No. He won. For the next seventy years, that poison went into almost every car on Earth.

NARRATOR: This is the story of leaded petrol. And if you grew up before it was banned, there's a good chance you breathed it.

NARRATOR: Let's go back to 1921. Cars have a problem. The engines knock.

SAM: Like, someone's at the door?

NARRATOR: Like a hammer inside the engine. The fuel is supposed to burn at exactly the right moment. But cheap fuel goes off too early, while the piston is still on its way up. The engine shudders, wastes power, and makes that knocking sound.

NARRATOR: General Motors puts a young engineer on the problem. Thomas Midgley.

MIDGLEY: Leave it to me. I'll try everything.

NARRATOR: And he does. He works his way across the periodic table, element after element.

MIDGLEY: Not that one. Not that one. Ooh. Getting warmer.

NARRATOR: In December 1921, he finds it. A compound called tetraethyl lead. Add a little to the fuel, and the knocking disappears.

MIDGLEY: Smooth engines! More power! And it's cheap!

NARRATOR: There's just one problem. And it's right there in the name.

SAM: ...Lead?

NARRATOR: Lead.

NARRATOR: People already knew lead was poisonous. Midgley had been warned about it back in 1922. He'd felt it himself.

NARRATOR: But the product went on sale anyway. With a friendlier name. Ethyl.

SAM: Rebranding. Classic.

NARRATOR: Then came October 1924. At a Standard Oil refinery in Bayway, New Jersey, the workers making the stuff started to change. They babbled. They lost their memory. They had sudden bursts of rage. Some collapsed in convulsions.

NARRATOR: Thirty-two of the forty-nine workers ended up in hospital. Five of them died.

NARRATOR: The workers had their own name for the building.

SAM: Please don't say it.

NARRATOR: The loony gas building.

NARRATOR: And the company's first explanation? The men had probably been working too hard.

SAM: [scoffs] Oh, come on.

NARRATOR: New York City, New Jersey and Philadelphia banned it. Production stopped. And that same month, Midgley held his press conference. The hands. The bottle. The sixty seconds.

MIDGLEY: I'm not taking any chance whatever.

NARRATOR: In 1925, the US government held a national conference. In 1926, its report found "no danger", as long as the factory workers were protected. The bans were lifted. Leaded petrol was back.

NARRATOR: But here's what nobody was measuring. When leaded petrol burns, the lead doesn't disappear. It comes out of the exhaust pipe as fine dust.

NARRATOR: Into the air. Onto the roads. Into the soil. Into the lungs of anyone nearby. Especially children.

SAM: How much are we talking?

NARRATOR: For decades, nobody really knew. And the man who found out wasn't even looking for it.

NARRATOR: Meet Clair Patterson. A geochemist with a very big question.

PATTERSON: How old is the Earth?

NARRATOR: His plan was to measure tiny amounts of lead in ancient rock, which works like a natural clock. But his results kept coming out wrong. There was lead in everything. Far more than there should have been.

PATTERSON: It's in the air. It's on the glassware. It's in the dust. It's on me.

NARRATOR: So he built one of the first super-clean laboratories, scrubbed everything, and finally got his answer.

PATTERSON: Four point five five billion years.

NARRATOR: That's still almost exactly the number scientists use today. Most people would have celebrated and moved on. Patterson had a new question.

PATTERSON: Where is all this lead coming from?

NARRATOR: He measured the ocean off California. Plenty of lead at the surface. It faded away the deeper he went. It was falling out of the sky. And he knew what was putting it there.

PATTERSON: Car exhausts.

NARRATOR: Some of his research money came from the oil industry. When his lead findings came out, industry leaders pressured him to stop, and threatened to take their money away.

SAM: So did he stop?

PATTERSON: No.

NARRATOR: In 1965, he published the evidence. He paid for it in his career. But the question was out, and it wasn't going back in.

NARRATOR: Slowly, the world caught up. In the 1970s, America started phasing leaded petrol out. On the first of January 1996, it was finally banned for cars.

NARRATOR: And by then, the government reported that the amount of lead in American children's blood had fallen by seventy percent.

SAM: Seventy?!

NARRATOR: The rest of the world took longer. In 2002, a hundred and seventeen countries were still using leaded petrol. The very last one, Algeria, stopped in July 2021.

NARRATOR: The United Nations says ending it will prevent more than one point two million early deaths. Every year.

SAM: Okay. But it's over now. Right?

NARRATOR: The petrol is gone. The damage isn't.

NARRATOR: In 2022, researchers worked out that more than a hundred and seventy million Americans alive today were exposed to high levels of lead as young children. About half the country.

NARRATOR: Lead harms the growing brain. Their estimate of the total cost: eight hundred and twenty-four million IQ points. Nearly three points per person, on average.

SAM: Hold on. Who got it the worst?

NARRATOR: People born in the mid-to-late 1960s. They may have lost up to six points each.

SAM: My dad was born in sixty-seven.

SAM: [pause] ...That explains his driving.

NARRATOR: And Thomas Midgley? He wasn't finished. A few years later, he took on another problem. Fridges used gases that could explode, or poison the people in the kitchen. Midgley helped create a replacement. Not toxic. Not flammable.

MIDGLEY: Chlorofluorocarbons! The miracle gas!

NARRATOR: CFCs. Decades later, scientists discovered that CFCs were eating away the ozone layer, the shield in the sky that blocks the Sun's harshest light.

SAM: No way. Same guy?

NARRATOR: Same guy. The historian J. R. McNeill wrote that Midgley "had more impact on the atmosphere than any other single organism in Earth's history".

SAM: That's... a terrible thing to be the best at.

NARRATOR: Here's the strangest part of this whole story. Leaded petrol didn't get banned because it stopped working. It worked perfectly. Smooth engines. Cheap fuel. It just took us decades to measure what it was really costing.

NARRATOR: So the next time somebody tells you a new chemical is perfectly safe...

MIDGLEY: Perfectly safe! I could do this every day!

NARRATOR: ...ask who measured it. And who's paying for the measuring.

SAM: And maybe don't sniff it.

- "Seventy years" is right for the US (1920s to the 1996 ban); worldwide it lasted until 2021, nearly a hundred years.
- That CFCs damaged the ozone layer is well established (it led to the 1987 Montreal Protocol), but it is not in the sources above; add a NASA or UNEP source for the description.
- "That's still almost exactly the number scientists use today": the accepted age is about 4.54 billion years; confirm with a geology source.
- Sam's dad is a joke character, not data.
